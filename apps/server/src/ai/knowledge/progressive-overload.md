# Progressive Overload

Progressive overload is the gradual increase of stress placed on the body during training. It is the primary driver of long-term strength and muscle gains: without a rising demand over time, the body has no reason to keep adapting.

Overload can be applied in several ways, not just by adding weight to the bar. Increasing reps at the same weight, adding an extra set, shortening rest periods, improving range of motion, or increasing training frequency for a muscle group are all valid forms of overload. Mixing these levers avoids relying on load increases alone, which eventually stalls for most lifters.

A practical rule of thumb: if a lifter hits the top of their target rep range for all working sets across two consecutive sessions, it is reasonable to increase the load. Typical increments are small — around 2.5kg for upper-body pressing and pulling movements, and 5kg for lower-body compound lifts like squats and deadlifts — because large jumps often cause a temporary drop in reps that erases the apparent progress.

Progress is not linear. Weekly fluctuations from sleep, stress, and nutrition are normal. What matters is the trend across several weeks, not any single session.
